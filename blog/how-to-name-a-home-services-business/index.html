Naming a business

How to name a home services business, and pick a domain that fits it.

A roofer's name has to work in places a software brand never sees: a truck door at forty miles an hour, a yard sign, a rushed phone call, an invoice. Here is a practical way to choose a home services name and a domain that fits it, with the rules from Google and the state that most people find out about too late.

Where a home services name has to work

Most naming advice is written for apps. A painter, fence builder, mover or house cleaner has a different set of tests, and the name either passes them or quietly costs calls for years:

  • The truck and the yard sign. Read at a distance, often for a second or two. Short words, no hyphens, nothing that needs explaining.
  • The phone. Customers hear your name from a neighbour and type it later. If they get it wrong, the call goes to whoever ranks for the misspelling.
  • Google Maps. Your profile name has rules (more on that below), and it must match what is on the truck.
  • The paperwork. Licences, insurance certificates, invoices and your state filing should all carry the same name.

Three kinds of name, and what each costs you

Your own name, as in Ortega Plumbing. Personal, trustworthy, easy to protect. The cost: it says nothing about where you work, unusual surnames are hard to spell, and it can be harder to sell the business later because the name is you.

A brand, a memorable word or phrase. Flexible and easier to protect. The cost: it takes longer for people to connect the name with the trade, so you spend more on signage and ads early on.

A descriptive name, the service plus the place, such as a deck builder name for one Denver suburb. It explains itself on a truck and a phone call. The cost: it is weak as a trademark, and it can box you in. A company named for decks that later adds fences, or for one suburb that expands across the metro, may outgrow the name.

A common answer is to combine them: a short company name that can grow, and a descriptive domain or two that point at the matching service pages. Our keyword or brand helper walks through the trade-off in six questions.

The Google Maps rule most owners learn too late

Google's guidelines for representing your business say your Business Profile name should reflect your real-world name, as used consistently on your storefront, website and stationery and as known to customers. They also say including unnecessary information in the name is not permitted and can lead to suspension.

That has a direct consequence for naming. If you want the words roofing or Denver to appear in your Maps listing, they have to be part of your genuine business name, on the truck, the invoices and your filing. You cannot run as Smith Construction everywhere else and call yourself Denver Roof Repair Experts on Google. Decide early which name you will actually trade under, because changing it later means new signage, new filings and a profile that has to be re-verified.

File the name with your state

Most states require you to register a name you trade under if it is not your legal name. In Colorado, the Secretary of State trade name FAQ explains that a for-profit business using a name other than its true entity name, or an individual using a name other than their legal first and last name, must file a statement of trade name. The same page notes that a trade name cannot be reserved in advance.

A trade name filing is not a trademark. The Colorado Secretary of State's trademark FAQ treats trademarks as a separate filing, and neither one is a federal registration. If you plan to grow beyond one market, look at the USPTO database too. The USPTO explains why to search for similar trademarks: names can conflict when they are similar and the services are related, even if they are not identical. Two fence companies with near-identical names in the same metro is exactly the confusion the law cares about, and it is bad for business even when nobody sues.

What the domain does, and does not, do

A city-and-service domain will not rank you on its own. Google's SEO Starter Guide says keywords in a domain name alone have hardly any effect beyond appearing in breadcrumbs. Rankings come from the site, the service pages, reviews and a consistent profile. We cover the details in exact-match domains for local businesses.

What a descriptive domain genuinely does is make you easy to remember and easy to type. Someone who saw a truck that said a painter name plus the town can find it again. That is worth a lot in a trade where most work comes from referrals, signs and repeat customers.

Whatever you choose, run it through the phone test. Say it once; can a stranger type it? Our domain name checker scores hyphens, numerals, length and word count, and this article on the phone test explains why each one matters.

Putting the domain on the truck

Once you have a domain, it should be on every vehicle, sign and invoice, and it should be printed the way people will type it. Leave off the www and the https; neither is needed and both make the line longer. If the domain joins several words, consider capitalising the start of each word on the truck so it reads at a glance, even though people will type it in lower case. Keep the phone number and the domain on the same panel, large enough to read from the next lane.

Then make sure the address goes somewhere useful. A visitor who typed your domain from a van has one question: can you do my job, where I live, soon? Send them to a page that answers that, with your service area and a phone number at the top, not to a generic homepage slideshow.

Avoid the near-copy

When the obvious name is taken, the temptation is to bolt on a letter, a hyphen or a different suburb and use something that looks almost the same as a competitor's. Customers will mix you up, reviews will land on the wrong business, and you may invite a trademark complaint. Pick something clearly your own. If the name you want is held by someone who is not using it, buying it properly is often cheaper than years of confusion.

A worked example

Imagine a painter starting out in Arvada, Colorado. She is weighing three options: her own surname plus Painting, a brand word, or a descriptive name for painters in Arvada.

  • She expects to work across the west metro within two years, so a name tied only to Arvada would be limiting as the company name.
  • Her surname is easy to spell, so she trades under it, files the trade name with the state and puts it on the van and her Google profile.
  • She also secures a descriptive painters-and-town domain and redirects it to her Arvada page, so people who remember the van can still find her.

One trading name everywhere, one website, one extra door. Nothing that breaks Google's rules, nothing she will outgrow.

A short checklist

  1. Decide which name you will genuinely trade under, on the truck, the invoices and Google.
  2. Check that it will still fit if you add services or towns.
  3. Search your state's business records and the USPTO database for similar names in your trade.
  4. File the trade name if it differs from your legal name.
  5. Choose the domain: the trading name if you can get it, plus a descriptive door if it helps people find you.
  6. Run the phone test, then put the domain on auto-renew with the lock on.

Straight answers

Questions this raises.

Does my home services business name have to match my Google Business Profile?

Yes. Google's guidelines say the profile name should reflect your real-world name as used on your storefront, website and stationery, and that adding unnecessary information can lead to suspension.

Do I need to register a trade name in Colorado?

According to the Colorado Secretary of State, a for-profit business using a name other than its true entity name, or an individual using a name other than their legal name, must file a statement of trade name.

Will a city-and-service domain help my business rank?

Not by itself. Google's SEO Starter Guide says keywords in a domain name alone have hardly any effect. A descriptive domain mainly helps people remember and type your address.

Should I name my company after my town?

Only if you expect to stay there. A town in the company name can limit you when you expand; a town in a secondary domain that redirects to a location page does not.
