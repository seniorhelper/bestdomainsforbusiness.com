AI names

How to choose a domain name for an AI startup.

Every AI founder hits the same three questions in the first week: .ai or .com, whether the word AI belongs in the name, and whether to describe the product or invent a brand. Here is how to answer each one with sources rather than hunches.

Start with what the name has to do

Before you look at a single extension, write down who has to find you and how. An AI product sold self-serve to developers lives and dies on a short, memorable brand that people pass around in chat and code. An AI service sold to local businesses, such as setting up agents for an accounting firm or running AI tools for a marketing team, usually wins on a name that explains itself on a phone call. Those are different jobs, and the right domain for one is often wrong for the other.

Three questions narrow it quickly:

  • Who types it? Technical buyers tolerate an unusual spelling; a busy owner hearing your name on a podcast will not.
  • Will the product change? Most AI products pivot at least once. A name tied to one feature can age badly.
  • Is this the company or a campaign? A company name needs to last; a campaign or lead-generation name can be literal and temporary.

The .ai versus .com question

It helps to know what .ai actually is. According to the IANA root zone database, .ai is the country-code top-level domain of Anguilla, and its manager of record is the Government of Anguilla. It became the default AI extension because of the letters, not because of anything technical.

For search, the extension matters less than people fear. Google's guide to multi-regional and multilingual sites lists .ai among the country-code extensions it treats as generic, meaning it is not read as a signal that your site targets Anguilla (Google notes that list can change). The SEO Starter Guide goes further and says the extension only matters if you are targeting a specific country's users, and even then it is usually a low-impact signal.

Where the extension does matter is people. The Verisign Domain Name Industry Brief still ranks .com as the largest top-level domain by registrations, and that habit shows up in typing and email. If you build on yourbrand.ai and someone else owns yourbrand.com, some of your visitors and some of your email will go to them. That is a nuisance if the .com is an unrelated parked page and a real problem if it is a competitor.

Two practical notes on .ai itself. Policies for a country-code extension are set by its registry rather than by the contracts that govern .com, so read your registrar's terms for .ai registration periods, renewals and transfers instead of assuming they match. And put renewals on auto-pay with a backup card; losing a startup's main domain to an expired card is an avoidable disaster. Our guide should your business domain be a .com? covers the trade-off for every common extension.

Should AI be in the name at all?

Putting AI in the name gives an instant category signal. Right now that is useful: a buyer scanning a list knows what you do. It also has three costs worth weighing.

  • Trademark strength. The USPTO's page on strong trademarks explains that descriptive marks are weak and harder to protect, while invented and arbitrary words are the strongest. A name that is mostly the word AI plus a generic noun is close to descriptive.
  • Crowding. Many new companies are making the same move, so an AI-plus-noun name can blur into its neighbours in search results, app stores and conversation.
  • Shelf life. If AI becomes as assumed in software as the internet is, a name built on the label may feel dated. That is a judgement call, not a prediction.

One thing AI in the domain will not do is rank you. Google's starter guide says keywords in a domain name alone have hardly any effect beyond appearing in breadcrumbs. Choose the word for people, not for an algorithm.

Descriptive, brandable, or both

There are three workable patterns for an AI company, and each fits a different business:

  • Descriptive. The name is the service: AI agent setup, AI for accounting firms, learning to use AI agents. Best for service businesses, courses and lead-generation sites, because the name answers the caller's first question.
  • Brandable. An invented or borrowed word with no keyword to carry. Best for venture-backed products expected to grow beyond one feature, and strongest for trademark purposes.
  • Hybrid. A short brand word plus a hint of the category. A good compromise when you need memorability and a clue to what you do.

Clear the trademark before you fall in love

AI naming has a specific trap: names that lean on a famous model, lab or product. They feel clever, attract attention, and invite a letter from a legal department. The USPTO explains why to search for similar trademarks before you commit: an application can be refused for likelihood of confusion with an existing mark when the names are similar and the goods or services are related, and they do not need to be identical to count. The USPTO also says the examiner only checks the federal database, so state registrations and ordinary internet use are your job to search.

If someone else owns a domain that copies your registered mark in bad faith, ICANN's Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy is the usual route for generic extensions. The same process can be used against you if your name copies theirs. A short session with a trademark attorney before launch costs far less than a rebrand after it.

Run the phone test and the spelling test

AI brands love dropped vowels, doubled letters and invented endings. Each one adds a chance that someone types the name wrong. Say the name once to a friend who has never heard it and ask them to type it. If they hesitate, ask whether the cleverness is worth the leak. Our domain name checker scores length, hyphens, numerals, word count and clarity, and shows every rule it applies, and picking a name that passes the phone test explains the thinking behind it.

Check the name's past

Plenty of short, AI-flavoured names have been registered before, sometimes for projects that left a poor reputation behind: spam, scraped content or an abandoned crypto token. Look at the Wayback Machine, run a site: search and check the registration record before you pay. Our walk-through, how to check a domain's history, takes about ten minutes.

Plan a small, sensible set

Once you have the main name, consider the .com or .ai counterpart if it is available, and the most likely misspelling. Point them at your main site with permanent redirects. Resist building separate thin sites on each variation; Google's spam policies describe many near-identical sites aimed at small variations of a search as doorway abuse. Our article on owning more than one domain covers the setup.

A one-page checklist

  1. Decide whether this is a company name, a product name or a campaign name.
  2. Pick a pattern: descriptive for services, brandable for products, hybrid if you need both.
  3. Choose the extension for people, then check who owns the other one.
  4. Search the USPTO database, state records and the open web for similar names in related fields.
  5. Run the phone test and the checker.
  6. Check the domain's history before paying.
  7. Secure the counterpart and the obvious misspelling, and redirect them.

Straight answers

Questions this raises.

Is a .ai domain bad for SEO?

No. Google lists .ai among the country-code extensions it treats as generic, and its SEO Starter Guide says the extension is a low-impact signal at most. The bigger risks with .ai are visitors typing .com by habit and renewal terms you have not read.

Should my AI startup put AI in its domain name?

It is a fair choice for a service business that wants an obvious category signal. For a product that may grow or pivot, a brandable name is usually easier to protect as a trademark and less likely to date.

Do I need to own both the .ai and the .com?

Not always, but check who owns the one you do not buy. An unrelated parked page is a minor leak; a competitor on the other extension is a reason to choose a different name.

How do I check whether an AI name is already trademarked?

Search the USPTO's federal trademark database, your state's records and the open web for similar names used for related goods or services. A trademark attorney can run and interpret a full clearance search.
