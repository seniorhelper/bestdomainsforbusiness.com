Legal names

Choosing a domain name for a law firm: the ethics rules, the practical tests and what lasts.

A law firm's domain is not just marketing. Under the ABA's model rules it counts as a professional designation, which means the same not-misleading standard that applies to your letterhead applies to your web address. Here is how to pick one that passes both the ethics test and the phone test.

Your domain is a professional designation

The ABA's comment to Model Rule 7.1 says firm names, letterhead and professional designations are communications about a lawyer's services. It allows a firm to be designated by the names of some or all of its current members, by the names of deceased members where there has been a succession in the firm's identity, or by a trade name that is not false or misleading. It then adds the line that matters here: a lawyer or law firm may also be designated by a distinctive website address, social media username or comparable professional designation that is not misleading.

In plain terms, your domain is judged like your firm name. Two caveats before going further. First, the model rules are a template; your state's adopted version is what binds you, and some states restrict trade names more tightly than the model rule does. Second, this article is practical guidance from people who sell domains, not legal advice. If a name feels borderline, ask your state bar's ethics hotline before you buy it.

What makes a law firm name misleading

The same ABA comment lists the classic problems. A firm name or designation is misleading if it implies a connection with:

  • a government agency,
  • a deceased lawyer who was not a former member of the firm,
  • a lawyer not associated with the firm or a predecessor firm,
  • a nonlawyer, or
  • a public or charitable legal services organization.

It also flags geographic trade names. Its example is a name like Springfield Legal Clinic, which may need an express statement that the firm is not a public legal aid organization. Translate that to domains and a few patterns deserve a second look: anything with words like legal aid, public defender, county or state that could suggest an official body, and anything that borrows the name of a lawyer who is not at your firm.

Superlatives are the other trap. The comment to Rule 7.1 warns that an unsubstantiated claim or comparison about a lawyer's services can be misleading if it is presented with enough specificity that a reasonable person would think it can be substantiated. A domain that calls a firm the best or the top in a city is a claim. Whether a given name crosses the line depends on your jurisdiction, so check before you build a practice on it.

The three patterns that work

Partner surnames. The traditional choice, and the most clearly permitted. It is also the most fragile online: surnames are often hard to spell after hearing them once, long multi-partner names make long domains, and a name partner leaving raises questions under your state's rules. If you go this way, consider a short form people actually say.

Practice area plus place. Names in the form of a practice and a city, such as a DUI attorney or divorce lawyer name for one town, explain themselves to a caller and are easy to remember from a radio spot or a bus bench. They are descriptive, so they are weak as trademarks, and they tie you to one practice and one market. Many firms use one as a second front door that redirects to the main site.

A trade name. Permitted in many jurisdictions if it is not misleading, and the most flexible as a brand. Check your state's version of the rule first, and make sure the trade name appears consistently on letterhead, signage and filings, not only in the web address.

You can see real examples of the second pattern on our legal and mediation domain names page, including a cluster of Greeley, Colorado lawyer names. Every one is owner-direct and negotiable.

A note for solo practitioners

Solo lawyers have the simplest path and often overthink it. Your own name plus a short word such as law or the practice area is clearly permitted under the model rule's approach to member names, is easy to say in court and on the phone, and moves with you if you later join or start a firm. If your full name is long or often misspelled, test a shorter form, such as first initial and surname, and redirect the full version to it.

What to avoid as a solo: a name that sounds like a large firm with several partners when there is one lawyer, or a plural like attorneys or group that could suggest colleagues you do not have. Under a not-misleading standard, a domain that overstates the size of a practice is exactly the kind of thing to check with your state bar first.

The practical tests

Once a name clears the ethics question, it still has to work in the real world. Clients often hear a firm's name in a stressful moment, from a friend, a referral or a phone call, and then type it from memory.

  • The phone test. Say it once and see whether someone can type it. Hyphens, numerals, doubled letters at word breaks and unusual surname spellings all leak clients. Our domain name checker shows exactly where a name loses points.
  • Length. Shorter is easier to say, print and type on a phone. A practice-and-city name can still be short if both words are common.
  • Extension. Google's SEO Starter Guide says the extension only matters for country targeting, and even then it is a low-impact signal. People still type .com by habit, so if you choose another extension, check who owns the .com version. If any legal-specific extension interests you, read its registry's eligibility rules first.
  • Email. You will read this domain aloud in every email address you give out. If it is awkward to spell for a court clerk, it is the wrong domain.

Do not rename your Google profile to match the domain

A descriptive domain does not change what you can call yourself on Google Maps. Google's guidelines for representing your business say the profile name should reflect the business's real-world name as used on its storefront, website and stationery, and that adding unnecessary information to the name is not permitted and can lead to suspension. If your firm is Martinez & Cole, your profile is Martinez & Cole, even if your redirect domain says DUI lawyer Greeley.

That is a reason to choose the main domain to match the name you actually practise under, and to use descriptive names as secondary doors rather than as a back door to a keyword-stuffed listing.

One site, several doors

Firms with several practice areas are often tempted to build a separate small site on each descriptive domain. Two problems. Every one of those sites is attorney advertising and has to meet your state's rules. And a set of thin, near-identical sites aimed at slight variations of the same search is what Google's spam policies call doorway abuse. The cleaner setup is one strong main site with a genuinely useful page for each practice area, and any extra domains pointed at the matching page with permanent redirects. Our article on owning more than one domain shows how to set that up.

Before you buy

  1. Check your state's version of Rule 7.1 and any ethics opinions on trade names and web addresses.
  2. Search the USPTO trademark database and your state's business records for similar names in legal services.
  3. Check the domain's past on the Wayback Machine and with a site: search. A law firm does not want to inherit a spam history; our history checklist takes ten minutes.
  4. Confirm the name works on letterhead, a business card, an email signature and a voicemail greeting.
  5. Put it on auto-renew with the registrar lock and two-factor login on.

Straight answers

Questions this raises.

Can a law firm use a domain name that is not the firm's name?

Under the ABA model rule, a firm may be designated by a distinctive website address that is not misleading. Your state's version of the rule controls, so check it and any related ethics opinions before you rely on a descriptive or trade-name domain.

Is it allowed to use best or top in a law firm domain?

It is risky. The comment to ABA Model Rule 7.1 says unsubstantiated claims or comparisons can be misleading. Whether a particular name crosses the line depends on your jurisdiction, so ask your state bar before buying one.

Can I put my practice area in my Google Business Profile name?

Only if it is genuinely part of your real-world business name. Google's guidelines say adding unnecessary information to the name is not permitted and can lead to suspension.

Should a law firm have a separate website for each practice area?

Usually not. One main site with a strong page for each practice area is easier to keep compliant, and extra domains can redirect to those pages instead of hosting thin copies.
