How to Check a Domain's History Before You Buy It

A domain can have a past you did not choose. Five free checks, about an hour in total, tell you what the name was used for before you pay for it.

Why a domain's past is your problem now

A domain name is not new just because it is new to you. Many good names have been registered, used, dropped and registered again, sometimes several times. Whatever happened under the name before you bought it can follow it: old links from spam sites, an email reputation that lands your messages in junk folders, or a trademark owner who has been waiting for someone to use it.

None of this is common enough to panic about, and all of it is checkable in under an hour with free tools. Here is the order we run the checks in.

1. Look at old versions in the Wayback Machine

The Internet Archive's Wayback Machine at web.archive.org stores snapshots of public websites going back decades. Type the domain in and you get a calendar of captures. Click through a few from different years.

What you are looking for is simple. A real local business, a personal blog or a parked page is fine. Pages stuffed with pills, casino links, foreign-language keyword lists or adult content are a warning that the name may have been used for spam, and that backlinks and blocklist entries may still exist.

No snapshots at all is not bad news. It usually means the name was never developed.

2. See what Google has indexed

Search Google for site: followed by the domain, with no space. If the name is not currently hosting a site, you should see nothing or very little. If you see hundreds of pages of strange content, the name is either still in use or Google still remembers something you want to understand before you pay.

Also search the bare domain in quotes. Mentions on forums, review sites or scam-warning pages are worth reading.

3. Read the registration record

ICANN's lookup tool at lookup.icann.org shows the registrar, the creation date and the expiry date. Our who owns this domain page walks through it.

The creation date matters in two ways. If it is very recent, the 60-day transfer restriction may apply, which we cover in how a domain transfer actually works. If the creation date is recent but the Wayback Machine shows years of history, the name has dropped and been re-registered at least once. That is normal, but it is a reason to look harder at the earlier checks.

4. Check the blocklists

If you plan to send email from the domain, its reputation matters. Google's Transparency Report has a Safe Browsing site status check that tells you whether Google currently flags the domain as unsafe. Spamhaus, one of the most widely used blocklist operators, offers a free lookup for domains on its Domain Blocklist.

A listing is not always fatal, since many are removed once the abuse has stopped, but it is something to resolve, or price in, before the money moves.

5. Check trademarks

Search the name in the United States Patent and Trademark Office's trademark database, and in the database for any other country you sell into. A domain that matches a registered mark in the same line of business can be challenged under ICANN's Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy, usually called the UDRP. If the challenge succeeds, the name is transferred away and the price you paid is not returned by anybody.

Descriptive phrases, such as a service and a city, are much less exposed than invented brand-like words, but the search takes five minutes, so run it either way.

After it lands: two more checks

  • Verify it in Google Search Console as soon as you control the DNS. The Manual actions and Security issues reports tell you whether Google has anything on file for the property.
  • Look at who links to it. Search Console's Links report shows linking sites once data comes in. If a previous owner left a trail of spam links, you can ask Google to ignore them with the disavow tool, which Google recommends only when there is a real problem.

Straight answers

Questions this raises.

How can I see what a domain was used for in the past?

Search the domain in the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine at web.archive.org. It shows saved snapshots of the site over the years, which tells you whether the name hosted a real business, a parked page, or something you would not want attached to your brand.

Can a domain carry a Google penalty from a previous owner?

A manual action is applied to a site, and a new owner with new content can ask Google to reconsider. The practical risk is a link profile full of spam pointing at the name. Check the backlinks before you buy, and after the transfer verify the domain in Google Search Console and look at the Manual actions report.

Should I check trademarks before buying a domain?

Yes. If the name matches a registered trademark in your field, the trademark holder can challenge it under ICANN's Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy. A quick search of the USPTO trademark database before you pay is far cheaper than losing the name later.
