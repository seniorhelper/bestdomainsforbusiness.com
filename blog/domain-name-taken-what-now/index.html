Finding a name

The domain you wanted is taken. Here is what to do next.

A registered domain is not the end of the road. It is the start of a short checklist that usually ends with either that name, a better one, or a cheaper one you had not thought of.

First, find out what “taken” actually means

When a registrar says a name is unavailable, it only means somebody holds the registration. It tells you nothing about whether they use it, want it, or would sell it. Before you give up or start bidding, spend ten minutes finding out which of these you are looking at:

  • A working business. A real site, real reviews, a phone number. These owners rarely sell cheaply, and sometimes not at all.
  • A parked page or a for-sale page. Ads, a “this domain may be for sale” banner, or a marketplace landing page. The owner is an investor or a registrar program, and there is a price somewhere.
  • Nothing at all. The name does not load or shows a registrar placeholder. Often someone registered it for a project that never happened.

Each of those leads somewhere different, so do not skip this step.

1. Read the public record

Look the name up on ICANN Lookup (our who owns this domain page walks you through it). Most owner details are redacted for privacy now, but three fields still matter: the registrar, the creation date and the expiry date.

An old creation date with a working site means an established owner. A creation date from last month means somebody just grabbed it, and it may be subject to a transfer restriction for a while. An expiry date in the next few weeks is the interesting one, which brings us to the next option.

2. Check whether it is about to expire

Domains are rented a year or more at a time. When an owner forgets to renew, the name does not instantly become free. ICANN’s Expired Registration Recovery Policy gives the previous owner a window to renew after expiry, and most generic domains then pass through a redemption period (commonly 30 days) and a short pending-delete stage before they are released.

Two honest warnings. First, many registrars sell expiring names through their own auctions during that window, so a good .com often changes hands before it ever drops. Second, a “backorder” service can try to catch a name the moment it is released, but nobody can promise you will get it. A backorder is a cheap lottery ticket, not a plan.

3. Look for a listing you missed

Plenty of names are openly for sale and the owner simply has not made it obvious. Search the exact domain in quotes, check whether it appears on the big aftermarket marketplaces, and look at the parked page for a small “buy this domain” link. If there is a listed price, you now know the ceiling. If there is a “make offer” button, you know the owner is willing to talk.

4. Approach the owner directly, properly

If the name is not listed, you can still ask. Most failed approaches fail because the first message sounds either desperate or insulting. Decide your number before you make contact, keep the message short, and do not tell a stranger how much the name means to your business.

We wrote the full method, including how to find an owner behind privacy redaction and what to say, in how to buy a domain someone else owns. If you are not sure what the name is worth, read what a domain name is actually worth first, and pay through escrow when you do agree a price (how transfers and escrow work).

5. Add a word that makes it better, not just available

The lazy fix is to bolt something onto the name until a registrar says yes. The good fix is to add a word that helps a customer. For a local service business, that word is usually the city or the service:

  • smithroofing.com is taken → smithroofingdenver.com tells people where you work.
  • brightsmile.com is taken → brightsmiledental.com tells people what you do.

Avoid modifiers that fail the phone test: numbers, hyphens, “4” for “for”, and doubled letters at word breaks. Run anything you shortlist through our domain name checker, which shows every rule it scores against.

One caution: if the taken name belongs to a competitor in your own field and town, a near-copy will confuse customers and may create trademark trouble. Pick something clearly your own.

6. Consider a different extension, with your eyes open

The same word on .net, .co or a newer extension may be open. That can work, but every non-.com name leaks some visitors and email to whoever owns the .com, because people type .com by habit. Before you buy, look at who owns the .com version. If it is an unrelated hobby site, the leakage is a nuisance. If it is a competitor, walk away. Our guide should your business domain be a .com? covers how Google and customers treat each kind of extension.

7. Look for a better name that already exists

People fixate on one exact phrase and miss the ones next to it. The name you can buy today, outright, with no negotiation, may be stronger than the one you started with. That is the whole point of browsing a portfolio by trade and city rather than guessing at a registrar: you see real, available-to-buy names grouped the way customers think. Start with the city and state pages or a category such as home services.

8. Use the trademark route only when it truly applies

If you hold a trademark and someone registered a confusingly similar domain in bad faith, ICANN’s Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy (UDRP) exists for exactly that. It is not a way to take a descriptive name like a service and a city from someone who registered it first. Panels can and do rule against complainants who try that. If you think you have a real claim, talk to a trademark attorney, and check the USPTO trademark search for your own name before you build on it.

Five mistakes that make a taken name cost more

  • Leading with your story. Telling an owner you have just signed a lease, printed signs or filed paperwork under that name tells them you cannot walk away.
  • Emailing from your business address. If your signature shows a funded company, expect the asking price to reflect it. A plain personal address and a short note work better.
  • Naming the first number. If the owner has a listed price, you have your ceiling. If not, asking what they would accept is often more useful than opening with a figure.
  • Registering lookalikes first. Buying the hyphenated or plural versions before you approach the owner can look like a threat, and some owners respond by refusing to deal at all.
  • Paying outside escrow. Even a friendly, honest-sounding owner can have a payment reversed or a transfer go wrong. Escrow protects both sides and costs little compared with the risk.

None of these are dramatic, but each one moves the price in the wrong direction or makes a deal less likely. The calmest buyer usually gets the best result.

Putting it together: a sensible order

  1. Find out what the current use is.
  2. Read the registration record and note the expiry date.
  3. Look for a listing or a make-offer page.
  4. Shortlist two or three modifier versions that pass the phone test.
  5. Check what already exists in portfolios like ours.
  6. Only then decide whether the original name is worth pursuing, and what your walk-away number is.

Straight answers

Questions this raises.

What does it mean when a domain is taken but has no website?

Someone holds the registration but is not using it. They may be an investor, a business that never launched, or someone who forgot about it. It is often the easiest kind of owner to buy from, but check the record and approach carefully.

Can I get a domain when it expires?

Sometimes. After expiry the previous owner gets a window to renew, and most generic domains then go through a redemption period before release. Many good names are sold at registrar auctions during that window, so a backorder is never a guarantee.

Is it worth buying a taken domain?

It depends on what the name does for your business compared with the best alternative you could have today. Set a walk-away number before contacting the owner, and compare it with names you can buy outright.

Should I add a city to my domain name?

For a local service business, a city often makes a taken name clearer and more useful, as long as the result is still short enough to say and type correctly. Run it through a domain name checker before committing.
