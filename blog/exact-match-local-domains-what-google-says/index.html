Keyword domains

Exact-match domains for local service businesses: what Google actually says.

A name like denverroofrepair.com is attractive for obvious reasons. Here is what Google has put in writing about names like that, what they genuinely do for a local business, and how to use one without wasting it.

The short answer

An exact-match domain (a name that matches what people search, such as a service plus a city) does not earn a Google ranking by itself. Google says so plainly. It can still be a very good business asset, for reasons that have nothing to do with a ranking trick. Both of those things are true at once, and most of the bad advice about domains comes from people who only tell you one of them.

What Google has published

Google’s guide to its search ranking systems names an exact match domain system. It says the words in a domain name are one factor among many when Google decides whether content is relevant, and that the system exists so Google does not give too much credit to content hosted on domains designed to exactly match particular searches.

Google’s SEO Starter Guide goes further. It lists keywords in the domain name among the things not to focus on, advises picking a name that is best for your business, and says that from a ranking perspective the keywords in a domain name alone have hardly any effect beyond appearing in breadcrumbs.

Read those together and the message is clear: a keyword in the domain is not a shortcut. Content, usefulness, reputation and, for local searches, your Google Business Profile do the work.

How local ranking works instead

For map and local results, Google’s help page on how local results are ranked describes three main factors: relevance (how well a profile matches the search), distance (how far the business is from the searcher or the location in the search) and prominence (how well known the business is, including reviews and links). Your domain name is not on that list.

There is also a trap here. Google’s Business Profile guidelines say your profile name must reflect your real-world business name, as used on your signage and with customers, and they do not allow adding extra keywords or locations that are not part of that name. Owning denverroofrepair.com does not let you call your profile “Denver Roof Repair” unless that is genuinely your business’s name. If you want the name to match, the clean way is to make it your actual trading name, registered properly where you operate.

What an exact-match name genuinely does

So why do businesses still pay for these names? Because they do real work that a ranking tweak never could:

  • It says what you do before anyone clicks. In a list of results, in an email signature or on a yard sign, the name explains itself.
  • It survives the phone and the truck door. A plain service-and-city name is easy to say, hear and type. That matters for radio, referrals and vehicle wraps.
  • It is easy to remember. People who saw your van last week can find you again without remembering your brand.
  • People link to you with the words you want. When someone links using your domain as the text, that text describes your service naturally.
  • Nobody else can have it. There is exactly one of each. Owning it keeps a competitor from owning it.

None of those is a promise of rankings, and we will never sell a name on that basis. They are reasons a good name keeps paying for itself in every channel, not only search.

Three sensible ways to use one

As your main brand. If your business name is, or can become, the descriptive phrase, building your main site on the exact-match name is simple and strong. Put the work into the site: real service pages, real photos, reviews, and a fast mobile experience.

As a campaign address that redirects. A memorable name used on radio, print or a truck can point to the right page of your main site with a permanent (301) redirect. Google’s documentation on redirects explains the options. This is about memorability and tracking, not rankings.

As a second business. If you genuinely run a separate line, for example a roofing company that also does gutters, a separate site with its own unique content can make sense.

The one way not to use one

Do not buy ten city names and put near-identical pages on each one. Google’s spam policies describe doorway abuse: sites or pages created to rank for specific, similar searches that funnel people to the same place. A network of thin city sites is the textbook example, and it can damage your main site too. If you serve several towns, build genuinely useful location pages on one site. We cover the multi-domain question in more depth in should a business own more than one domain?

How to judge an exact-match name before you buy

  • Is the phrase what customers actually say? “Roofer” or “roofing”? “Attorney” or “lawyer”? Ask a few customers.
  • Does it pass the phone test? No hyphens, no numbers, no doubled letters at word breaks. Our domain name checker scores this openly.
  • Is it a .com? People type .com by habit.
  • Is the history clean? See how to check a domain’s history.
  • Will you still like it if you expand? A city name is a commitment to that city. A service name is a commitment to that service.

A worked example

Picture a roofing company in Aurora, Colorado called Peak & Sons. Its owner is offered auroraroofrepair.com. Here is how we would think it through with him.

What it will not do: it will not push Peak & Sons up the map results. Those depend on the company’s Business Profile, its reviews, its distance from the searcher and how well known it is locally. The profile must stay “Peak & Sons Roofing”, because that is the real business name.

What it can do: it is a perfect address for the side of a truck, a yard sign at every job, a radio spot and a postcard after hail season. People who see it remember it, and it tells them exactly what they get. Pointed with a permanent redirect at a strong “roof repair in Aurora” page on the main site, every visit lands where it should, and campaign tags in the destination address show how many came from the trucks.

The alternative: if the owner were starting fresh, he could trade as Aurora Roof Repair and build the main site on the name. That is a branding decision, not an SEO trick, and it has to be made properly, with the right business registration.

The decision: buy it if the price is in line with what a memorable name is worth across all those channels for years, not because someone promised a ranking.

Three myths worth dropping

  • “Exact-match domains were penalized, so they are worthless.” Google reduced the credit given to low-quality sites on matching names. A good name with a good site is not penalized for its name.
  • “A keyword domain is all the SEO you need.” The site, the content and the reviews do the work.
  • “More keywords in the name is better.” Long names fail the phone test and look spammy. Short and clear wins.

Where to look for one

The obvious city-and-service .com names for most trades were registered years ago, which is why they are worth something. Straight answers

Questions this raises.

Does an exact-match domain help local SEO?

Not by itself. Google's own documentation says keywords in a domain name have hardly any ranking effect on their own, and its exact match domain system is designed to avoid over-crediting such names. Local results depend on relevance, distance and prominence.

Can I use my keyword domain as my Google Business Profile name?

Only if it is genuinely your real-world business name. Google's Business Profile guidelines do not allow adding keywords or locations that are not part of your actual name.

Is an exact-match domain bad for SEO?

No. Google does not penalize a name for containing keywords. Problems come from thin or spammy content, or from networks of near-duplicate sites, not from the name itself.

Why would a local business pay for an exact-match domain?

Because it says what you do, is easy to say and remember, works on vehicles, radio and print, and can only be owned by one business. Those are business benefits, not ranking guarantees.
