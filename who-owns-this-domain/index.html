Reading the record

What the lookup is actually telling you.

Registrar

The company the name is registered through. Useful because it tells you where the name will have to be unlocked from when it transfers, and some registrars are far slower than others.

Creation date

How long the name has existed. Worth knowing, but do not overpay for age alone — Google has said domain age is not a ranking factor, and an old name with a bad history is worse than a new one.

Expiration date

Tells you when the owner next has to make a decision. It does not tell you the name is about to become available, because almost all of them renew.

Registrant, or a privacy shield

Most of the time this is a privacy service now, not a person. That is normal and it is not a sign the owner is hiding something. It just means the easy route is closed.

After you agree a price

How the transfer actually works.

  1. Agree terms in writing

    Name, price, who pays fees, and what is included. Use escrow on a meaningful purchase.

  2. Seller unlocks and sends the auth code

    The authorization (EPP) code proves control. It should be sent privately, never posted.

  3. Buyer starts the transfer

    At the buyer’s own registrar. The old registrar has a short window to release it — typically a few days for a .com.

  4. Lock it down

    Registrar lock on, auto-renew on, two-factor login on, contact details current.

The 60-day rules

Under ICANN’s Transfer Policy, a registrar may refuse a transfer to another registrar within 60 days of the domain’s initial registration or of a previous transfer. Separately, changing the registrant’s details can trigger a 60-day transfer lock at many registrars unless the registrant opted out beforehand.

Ask when the name was last registered or transferred before you set a closing date, and ask the seller not to edit contact details mid-deal. ICANN has been revising this policy, so confirm the current rules with the ICANN Transfer Policy and your registrar.

Ownership questions

The ones that come up every time.

How do I find out who owns a domain name?

Start with a public WHOIS lookup. ICANN Lookup is the official one and who.is is the most readable. Both are free and neither requires an account. You will see the registrar, the registration and expiration dates, and — if the owner has not enabled privacy protection — contact details.

Why does the lookup show a privacy service instead of a person?

Because most registrars now include privacy protection by default, and European and other privacy rules redact a lot of what used to be public. You will usually see a forwarding address at the privacy provider rather than a human. That address often works, but it is a slow channel and plenty of owners never check it.

Can I contact a domain owner who is behind privacy protection?

Sometimes. The privacy forwarder, the site itself if one exists, the business behind the name, and public business records are all legitimate routes. What you should not do is try to work around the privacy service through anything sketchy. If the honest routes fail, that is exactly what our acquisition service is for.

The domain expires next month. Should I just wait?

Usually a bad bet. Expiring names go through a renewal grace period, then a redemption period, then an auction, and the overwhelming majority get renewed by the owner before any of that finishes. Names worth having rarely fall out the bottom. Waiting mostly costs you months.

Someone owns a domain with my business name in it. What now?

That may be a trademark matter rather than a purchase. ICANN’s Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy exists for exactly this, and in the United States the Anticybersquatting Consumer Protection Act is the court route. We are not lawyers and this is not legal advice — if you think a name infringes a mark you hold, talk to a trademark attorney before you make an offer, because an offer can change the picture.
